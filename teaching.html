Teaching

Getting a room full of people to think out loud

Knowing something and getting a room of undergraduates to care about it are different skills. What a philosophy classroom still offers that nothing else does is live, unscripted discussion between people — and that, too, can be taught.

Philosophy of teaching

Four commitments

Design the room

Participation is mostly a product of the room — the one thing the instructor controls — so I design for it. I talk with students before class starts, check in about what's unclear, and often run discussion as Socratic circles: an inner circle discusses while an outer circle watches the shape of the conversation, then the circles switch and report. Students learn to see a discussion as a discussion, not a queue for their own opinion.

A role for every voice

My "keyword discussions" give students named roles to speak from — the Excavator, who surfaces an unstated premise; the Contrarian, who tests the argument; the Complexifier, who troubles a framed binary — eight in all, each performed by semester's end. The roles give strong opinions somewhere to go and give hesitant students a legitimate way in.

Make the standard explicit

I define participation precisely and keep a record of it, and I build assessment the same way: a tiered final exam that opens on day one (identify the theories, draw out their obligations, apply one to a case), and a scaffolded term paper with staged "prospective ideas" and a writing workshop — with an alternative for students the essay format doesn't serve.

Argue in good faith

I keep my own commitments out of the room. What I enforce is an argumentative standard: state the strongest version of the view you are arguing against, charitably enough that someone who holds it would recognize herself in it. That single rule is the thing I most want students to carry out with them — even if they never take another philosophy course.
