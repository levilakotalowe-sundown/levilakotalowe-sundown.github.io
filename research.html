What addiction is, what it is not

I am a philosopher of science. My dissertation is focused squarely on addiction. I discuss what addiction is, what it is not, and why those clarifications matter for psychiatry. I considered moments where someone would say, Your brain is not supposed to work like that, and I try to make sense of it. Sometimes I agree. Much of the time, as you will come to read, I do not.

Research statement (PDF) Writing sample (PDF)

Briefly, my positive argument is as follows: Agent A depends on X to achieve goal G. From there, I argue that, when we are talking about agents, dependencies may either succeed or fail. A dependence is successful when an agent (A) depends on X to achieve G, actually achieves G through X, and there are no unacceptable consequences. Common examples would be: drinking water for hydration, wearing shoes to protect one's feet, or using an EpiPen to prevent anaphylaxis. Unsuccessful dependence, by contrast, is failure either in the main goal pursuit or in the acceptability of the consequences. Reasonable examples, respectively, could be: depending on Ibuprofen to cure viral infections, or depending on a knife to cut through carrots but cutting through one's finger as well. I argue that this more evaluatively neutral understanding of dependence in lieu of evaluatively negative uses—in discussions of addiction—improves our understanding of what counts as pathological use. In fact, it better aligns with contemporary philosophical and psychiatric addiction literature. I will conclude by suggesting that addiction is currently best understood, not as similar to dependence generally, but as persistent unsuccessful dependence (PUD).

We should flip the script, so to speak. Dependence and addiction are more like antonyms than synonyms.

The Addiction Game: Prevention and Unstable Preferences

Addictive behavior is often considered irrational. The argument goes: it does not make sense that one would continuously use something, like drugs or alcohol, that runs orthogonal to her preferences or otherwise frustrates her interests. It is this self-induced contradiction of preferences that philosophers and clinicians describe as "irrational." While there is opposing literature that posits a theory of rational addiction, the word "rational" here always refers to agents with forward-looking and stable preferences. Using a simple game theoretic setup, I show how even in cases involving unstable preferences, rational choices do not prevent addiction.

Keywords — rational decision making · game theory · addiction · preferences · prevention

Dissertation

Persistent unsuccessful dependence

Chapter I

Dependence Theory: Distinguishing Dependence from Pathological Use

Draft availableRead the draft (PDF)

Addiction is often associated with the term "dependence." In what follows, I explain that this association is a conflation that has caused serious problems, even contradictions, in how dependence has been characterized both socially and clinically. I show precisely how an evaluatively negative interpretation of dependence—one focused on subjugation and compulsion—misinformed addiction. Rather than being synonymous, I explain why current philosophical literature on addiction understands pathological use as instead contrasting dependence. Specifically, I use the phrase "persistent unsuccessful dependence" to describe the current model. Under this model of addiction, the ailment of pathological use would not be a case of too much dependence, but rather a case of having too much unsuccessful dependence. By transitioning from an evaluatively negative interpretation of dependence to an evaluatively neutral, and teleological, interpretation of it, I show how we can both capture intuitive cases of addiction and solve puzzling cases. Indeed, the goal of implementing this framework is to increase the accuracy of addiction diagnosis.

Chapter II

The Addiction Game: Prevention and Unstable Preferences

Draft availableRead the paper (PDF)Writing sample, above.

While Becker and Murphy (1988) do model addiction as a rational process, they also model addiction as a deterministic and cumulative process, where the agent maintains stable preferences over time. Those modeling choices are important because, under those assumptions, abstention is always (at least weakly) rational at the prevention stage. I model addiction as a probabilistic process, where the agent has unstable preferences. In doing so, we find cases wherein abstention is irrational at the prevention stage. The elimination of the strategic dominance of abstention at the prevention stage can explain, in some cases, how addiction can arise from rational choices. … Addicts suffer from irrational desires, not irrational decisions.

Chapter III

Addiction Is Not Adaptive Preference: A Feminist View on a Psychiatric Puzzle

In progressRead the current draft (PDF)

Imagine a person and call him Marty. Marty claims to successfully depend on alcohol. That is, he has a goal, uses alcohol to successfully achieve it, and accepts all the consequences involved. The success of the proclaimed dependence is rated purely teleologically. That is, the current model of alcohol use disorder (AUD) says that Marty does not have AUD because the alcohol-dependency here is teleologically successful. In this paper, I ask: does that understanding of addiction make sense? I answer: yes. … If a non-deceived individual claims to have a successful dependence, then it is inaccurate to diagnose her with addiction, even if the successful dependence counts as an adaptive preference because adaptive preferences are successful dependencies.

Future work

What makes something addictive?

Paper

What Makes Something Addictive?

Partly drafted

The main psychiatric reason that drugs became the defining example of addiction is known as the dopamine theory of addiction. However, since this theory's wider adoption, one behavioral addiction—namely gambling disorder—has also been accepted into the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM). Gambling's admission into the DSM shows the limiting criterion on addiction was never dopamine; it is some unacceptable cost together with the unwarranted prediction of future reward. That cost, importantly, must relate to the user's own goals, not the harm it may do to others. Addiction can of course cause harm to others, but harm to others never constitutes addiction outright. Addiction broadens beyond drugs, but it is bounded by patient-relativity. In this paper, I examine why drug addiction became the prototypical example of addiction, and why gambling disorder makes sense to include as a behavioral addiction, in order to make a case for a stronger overall demarcation of addiction from social critique.
