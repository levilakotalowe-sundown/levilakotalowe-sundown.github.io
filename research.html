Research

Rationality at the edge of addiction

I work in the philosophy of science, the philosophy of psychiatry, and decision theory. For example, one of my central questions is: if addiction is a failure of rationality, why doesn't staying rational keep it away?

Contemporary philosophy and psychiatry often describe addiction as irrational — a person keeps using something that contradicts their own preferences. That framing is intuitive, and it invites a tempting inference: if addiction is irrationality, then remaining rational should be a defense against it.

I think that inference is mistaken, and my research explains why. Rather than treating rationality as a bulwark, I model the run-up to addiction — the prevention stage, before any dependence is in place — as a decision problem for an agent whose preferences are unstable and whose future self is, in effect, a different player. Using tools from game theory and rational-choice theory, I show that perfectly rational, preference-aligned reasoning can still lead an agent toward addiction. The point is not that people reason badly; it is that reasoning well is not enough.

This project connects long-standing debates in the philosophy of science about modeling and idealization to live questions in the philosophy of psychiatry about diagnosis, agency, and responsibility — and it aims to be clear enough that students, clinicians, and philosophers can all follow the argument.

The Addiction Game: Prevention and Unstable Preferences

Addictive behavior is often considered irrational: it seems not to make sense that a person would continuously use something that runs orthogonal to her preferences or frustrates her interests. It is this self-induced contradiction of preferences that philosophers and clinicians describe as "irrational." While an opposing literature posits a theory of rational addiction, "rational" there always refers to agents with forward-looking, stable preferences. Using a simple game-theoretic setup, I show how — even in cases involving unstable preferences — rational choices do not prevent addiction.

Keywords — rational decision making · game theory · addiction · preferences · prevention

Dissertation

What addiction actually is

My dissertation develops a book-length account of addiction with a single guiding claim: addiction is not a breakdown in rational decision-making but a malfunction in the desire-formation systems that decision-making reads from. Understood this way, addiction is best modeled as persistent unsuccessful dependence — a reframing that sharpens who should, and who should not, be diagnosed. Across three chapters I move from what addiction is, to why rational choice can't prevent it, to the cases our current models risk getting wrong.

  1. Dependence Theory: Distinguishing Dependence from Pathological Use Draft availableAddiction is not synonymous with dependence. Tracing the concept through the DSM and recent philosophy of psychiatry, I replace an evaluatively negative reading of dependence with a neutral, teleological one — recasting addiction as "persistent unsuccessful dependence" to make diagnosis more accurate.
